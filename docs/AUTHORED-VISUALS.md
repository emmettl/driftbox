# User-authored visuals

Every existing touch interaction acts on material the scene supplied: it attracts,
distorts, turns or fans something out, then the scene restores itself. The missing
interaction is authorship — being able to point at the result after lifting a finger and
say “I made that.”

This exploration keeps the full-screen filter pad. Drawing therefore has two linked
consequences: the hand shapes the picture and performs the mix at the same time.

## The useful test

An authored interaction should pass all four:

1. The result is still legible after the finger lifts.
2. The input and result coincide on both a phone and a desktop.
3. The track does something with the result without taking ownership of its shape away.
4. A first gesture is rewarding without instructions.

## Directions

| Direction | Gesture | Lasting result | What the music does | Character |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Longhand** | Draw freely | Luminous tubing stays on a dark stave | Playheads travel every route; bass blooms the halo | Expressive, immediate |
| **Switchyard** | Join fixed terminals | A network of snapped signal routes | Hits are packets choosing the player's routes | More game-like, more rules |
| **Cut glass** | Slice a translucent sheet | Incisions reveal the bright layer behind it | Transients send cracks branching from cuts | Visceral, graphic |
| **Contour** | Draw closed or crossing lines | Lines raise permanent ridges into a landscape | Bass breathes the terrain; hats erode its peaks | Sculptural, slower |
| **Constellation** | Tap stars, drag between them | A player-named constellation | Notes illuminate edges in pitch order | Deliberate, accessible |

## First vertical slice: Longhand

Longhand is the cleanest way to test the premise because it has almost no grammar to
learn. A press begins a stroke, a drag shapes it, and release commits it. Up to twelve
strokes remain for the lifetime of the scene; only then does a new one replace the oldest.

The lines are scene geometry, not a longer-lived version of the filter-pad cursor. That
is important visually and conceptually: they sit behind the interface, carry depth, and
the music can travel along them. The track changes glow and sends a white reader along
each route, but it never moves the route itself.

Questions the prototype should answer:

- Does simultaneously drawing and sweeping the filter feel synaesthetic or restrictive?
- Is freehand enough, or does authorship need consequences such as routing and collision?
- Should marks survive a scene change, and if so are they local device state or part of a
  shared song?
- Does replacing the thirteenth-oldest stroke feel natural, or should there be an explicit
  clear gesture?

## Likely second prototype

Switchyard is the useful counterpoint. Longhand maximises expression; Switchyard
maximises consequence. Terminals would make a route easy to draw with a thumb and let the
scene visibly “use” the player's decisions. If Longhand feels decorative rather than
authored, Switchyard is the next experiment—not an embellished version of the same one.
